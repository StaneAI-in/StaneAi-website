What is an MVP? How to plan the first version of your app

By the StaneAI team · 27 November 2026

Almost every app idea starts with a long list of features. Login, profiles, payments, chat, notifications, an admin panel, reports, referrals, dark mode… It is tempting to build all of it before launch. The trouble is that a big first version takes longer, costs more and is built on guesses about what users want. An MVP — a minimum viable product — is the way out of that trap. This guide explains what an MVP really is, what it is not, and how to decide what goes into yours.

The short answer

An MVP is the smallest version of your product that solves one real problem well enough that real people will use it, so you can learn from them before you invest more. It is not a demo, and it is not a half-broken app. It is a complete, working product with a deliberately narrow scope.

  • Minimum — only the features needed for the core job. Everything else waits.
  • Viable — good enough that people actually use it, trust it and, ideally, pay for it.
  • Product — a real thing in users’ hands, not a slide deck or a mock-up.

What an MVP is not

The term is often misused, so it helps to be clear:

  • Not a prototype. A clickable prototype is great for testing an idea with a few people, but nobody can rely on it day to day. An MVP works with real data and real users.
  • Not a low-quality app. “Minimum” applies to the number of features, not to quality. The few features you ship should be fast, clear and reliable.
  • Not version 1 of your full roadmap. If your MVP plan contains every feature you have ever imagined, it is not an MVP.

Why start with an MVP?

  • You learn faster. Real usage tells you which features matter. Most teams are surprised by what users actually do.
  • You spend less before you know more. A narrow first version usually costs a fraction of a full build. See our guide on what drives app development cost.
  • You launch sooner. Fewer features means a shorter path to the app stores. Our realistic app timeline shows where the time goes.
  • You have something to show. Investors, partners and early customers respond far better to a working product with a few real users than to a plan.

How to decide what goes into your MVP

  1. Write the problem in one sentence. For example: “Small clinics lose patients because booking an appointment needs a phone call.” If you cannot write it in one sentence, the scope is not clear yet.
  2. Pick one type of user to serve first. A marketplace has buyers and sellers; a clinic app has patients, doctors and staff. Choose the user whose problem matters most and design for them first.
  3. Describe the core journey. List the steps that user takes from opening the app to getting the result they came for — for example: find a clinic → choose a time → confirm → get a reminder. Every screen in your MVP should serve this journey.
  4. Sort every feature into three buckets. Must have (the journey breaks without it), should have (useful, but users can manage without it at first) and later. Be strict: most features belong in the last two.
  5. Look for manual shortcuts. Many features can be done by a person behind the scenes at first. Instead of building a full admin dashboard, your team might approve sign-ups from a simple spreadsheet or a basic internal page. Automate it once you know it is needed.
  6. Decide how you will measure success. Choose two or three numbers before launch, such as how many people complete the core journey, how many come back in the second week, or how many pay. Add basic analytics so you can see them.

A worked example

Imagine an app that helps local gyms manage memberships. The full wish list might include class booking, payments, a workout tracker, diet plans, trainer chat, a referral programme and a members’ social feed. A sensible MVP could be:

  • members sign up and see their membership status and expiry date;
  • members book a class and get a reminder;
  • gym staff see the day’s bookings on a simple screen;
  • renewals are paid through one payment gateway.

Workout tracking, diet plans, chat and referrals wait until real gyms and members have used the app and told you what they miss. That MVP is still a polished, trustworthy product — it just does one job well.

Common MVP mistakes

  • Too many features. The most common mistake by far. Each extra feature adds design, development, testing and maintenance.
  • Skipping the basics that build trust. Security, reliable login, clear error messages and a privacy policy are not optional, even in a first version — especially if you handle payments or personal data.
  • No way to learn. Launching without analytics or a way to collect feedback wastes the main reason for building an MVP.
  • Throwaway code. Keep the scope small, but build the foundations properly — a clean backend, sensible data structure and good documentation — so version two extends the MVP instead of replacing it.
  • Waiting for perfect. If you are not slightly uncomfortable with how little the first version does, it probably does too much.

Should the MVP be a mobile app, a web app or both?

It depends on where your users are and how often they will use the product. Something people use daily on the go, or that needs notifications, camera or location, usually suits a mobile app. Tools used at a desk, or by a business team, often start as a web app. If you need both Android and iOS, a cross-platform framework keeps the cost down — our comparison of Flutter and React Native explains the options, and Android vs iOS covers which platform to start with if you launch on only one.

What happens after the MVP?

Launch is the start of the learning, not the end of the project. Talk to users, watch the numbers you chose, fix what frustrates people, and then move features from the “should have” and “later” lists into the next release — but only the ones real usage supports. A steady rhythm of small, regular updates usually beats one big second version.

Common questions

How long does it take to build an MVP?

It depends on the scope, but a focused app MVP often takes somewhere between a few weeks and a few months, including design, development, testing and store approval. The narrower the core journey, the faster it goes.

How much does an MVP cost?

Much less than a full product, because you build fewer screens, integrations and admin tools. The biggest factors are the number of user types, the integrations you need (payments, maps, chat) and how custom the design is.

Can an MVP use AI features?

Yes, if the AI feature is part of the core job — for example an AI chatbot that answers customer questions or an automation that reads documents. Keep it focused on one task, and plan for a human check where mistakes would be costly.

Do I need an MVP if I already know what users want?

Even with strong customer knowledge, a focused first release reduces risk and gets you to market sooner. You can still plan the full roadmap — you just build it in steps, guided by real usage.

The bottom line

An MVP is not about building less for the sake of it. It is about building the right thing first: one problem, one type of user, one core journey, done well. Launch it, learn from real people, and let that learning decide what you build next.
