How long does it take to build a mobile app? A realistic timeline

By the StaneAI team · 24 November 2026

After “how much will it cost?”, the next question almost every founder asks is “how long will it take?”. The honest answer depends on what the app needs to do, how clearly it is defined, and how quickly decisions get made on your side. This guide walks through each phase of a typical mobile app project, gives broad timeline ranges, and explains what usually slows things down — so you can plan a launch date you can actually hit.

The short answer

These are rough, indicative ranges for a first version, from the start of planning to the app being live in the stores. They are not promises, and your project may be faster or slower depending on scope, team size and how fast feedback flows.

  • Simple app (a few screens, basic forms, simple backend, no payments): often around 2 to 4 months.
  • Medium-complexity app (user accounts, payments, admin panel, notifications, a few integrations): commonly 4 to 8 months.
  • Complex app (multiple user roles, real-time features, marketplace logic, AI features, heavy integrations): 9 months or more, and usually best released in phases.

If someone promises a complex app in a few weeks, ask exactly what will be delivered in that time. It may be a prototype rather than a finished product.

The phases of building an app

Most app projects move through the same broad phases. Some of them overlap — testing, for example, should happen throughout development, not only at the end — but it helps to think about each one separately.

1. Discovery and planning (about 1 to 3 weeks)

This is where the idea becomes a plan: who the users are, what problem the app solves, which features belong in the first version and which can wait. A good outcome is a written scope with user flows, a feature list split into “must have” and “later”, and a rough technical approach. Skipping this phase rarely saves time; it usually moves the confusion into development, where it is more expensive to fix.

2. Design (about 2 to 6 weeks)

Designers turn the flows into wireframes (simple layouts) and then into finished screens. Apps built mostly from well-designed standard components sit at the shorter end. A fully custom visual style with illustrations and animations takes longer. The number of review rounds matters too: each round of feedback and changes adds days.

3. Development (about 6 to 20+ weeks)

This is the longest phase and the one most affected by scope. Development usually covers three parts that move in parallel:

  • The app itself — the screens your users see on Android and iOS.
  • The backend — the server, database and API that store data and run business logic.
  • The admin panel — where your team manages users, content and orders. It is easy to forget, and it often takes a meaningful share of the time.

Working in short cycles (often one or two weeks each) with a demo at the end of every cycle keeps the project visible. You see real progress, and problems surface early instead of in the final month.

4. Testing and fixes (about 2 to 4 weeks, plus ongoing)

Testing should run alongside development, but there is almost always a dedicated stretch before launch to test full user journeys on real devices, fix bugs and check performance. Android runs on a wide range of phones and screen sizes, so testing needs to cover more ground than a single test device.

5. App store submission (a few days to a couple of weeks)

Both Google Play and the Apple App Store review apps before they go live. Reviews are often quick, but they can take longer, and a rejection means fixing the issue and resubmitting. Some practical points to plan for:

  • Developer accounts take time to set up. Registering as an organisation can require business verification documents, which may take days or weeks. Start this early, during design or development.
  • Google Play has extra steps for some new accounts. Certain newly created developer accounts must run a closed test with a minimum number of testers for a set period before releasing publicly. Check the current Play Console requirements for your account type well before your target date.
  • Store listings need preparation. Screenshots, descriptions, privacy policy, data safety details and content ratings all need to be ready.

6. Launch and the first weeks after

Launch is not the finish line. The first few weeks usually bring bug reports from devices you did not test on, feedback from real users and small improvements. Plan for the team to stay available during this period rather than moving straight on to the next project.

What makes app projects take longer

Most delays are not caused by slow coding. They come from a handful of predictable sources:

  • Unclear or changing scope. Adding “just one more feature” mid-project affects design, development and testing. A few of these add up quickly.
  • Slow decisions and feedback. If design approvals or answers to questions take a week each, the project waits a week each time.
  • Third-party dependencies. Payment gateway approvals, API access from partners, or older systems with poor documentation can stall work you cannot speed up.
  • Content that is not ready. Product data, text, images and legal pages are often left until the end and then become the bottleneck.
  • Building two separate native apps. Separate Android and iOS codebases mean more work than one shared codebase, both now and in future updates.
  • Underestimating the backend and admin panel. The parts users never see are frequently where the hidden effort lives.

How to launch sooner without cutting corners

Start with an MVP

A minimum viable product is the smallest version of the app that solves the core problem for your first users. For each feature on your list, ask: “Would early users stop using the app if this was missing?” If not, move it to a later release. A smaller first version is the single most effective way to shorten a timeline. Our guide on mobile app development cost in India covers the same idea from the budget side.

Consider cross-platform development

Frameworks such as Flutter and React Native let one codebase run on Android and iOS. For many business apps — booking, e-commerce, content, internal tools — this shortens development compared with two native apps. If you would rather launch on one platform first, see Android vs iOS: which platform should you build your first app for?

Use proven services

Login, payments, push notifications, analytics and hosting are available as reliable managed services. Using them is usually faster and safer than building each one from scratch.

Prepare your side of the work

Nominate one person who can make decisions and answer questions quickly. Get your content, brand assets, developer accounts and payment gateway applications moving early. These small steps often save more time than any technical choice.

A sample timeline for a medium-complexity app

To make this concrete, here is how an illustrative medium-complexity app — say, a booking app with user accounts, payments, notifications and an admin panel, built cross-platform — might be laid out. It is an example for planning, not a commitment for any specific project.

  1. Weeks 1–2: discovery, scope and user flows.
  2. Weeks 3–6: wireframes and final designs; developer accounts and payment gateway set up in parallel.
  3. Weeks 5–18: development in short cycles with regular demos, starting with the backend and core flows.
  4. Weeks 17–20: full testing on real devices, bug fixes, store listings prepared.
  5. Weeks 20–22: store submission, review and launch.

That comes to roughly five months. Change the scope, the number of platforms or the speed of decisions, and the timeline changes with it.

Common questions

Can an app be built in a month?

A very small app or a clickable prototype can sometimes be built in a few weeks. A production-ready app with a backend, admin panel, testing and store approval usually takes longer. If you need something in a month, focus on a prototype to test the idea with real users.

How long do updates take after launch?

It depends on the change. Small fixes can ship within days, while new features go through the same design, build and test steps on a smaller scale. Plan for ongoing maintenance — operating system updates and security fixes arrive whether or not you add features.

What should I ask a development team about timelines?

  • What exactly is included in the timeline — design, backend, admin panel, testing, store submission?
  • What do you need from us, and by when?
  • How often will we see working progress?
  • How are scope changes handled, and how do they affect the date?
  • What happens in the weeks after launch?

The bottom line

Most first versions of a business app take somewhere between a few months and the better part of a year, depending mostly on scope. You have more control over the timeline than it may seem: keep the first version focused, decide quickly, prepare content and accounts early, and insist on regular demos. That combination gives you a launch date you can plan around.
